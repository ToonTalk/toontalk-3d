What it is
+

A workshop you can pick up

ToonTalk is a programming language from 1995 that looks like a video game. You do not write code in it; you go into an animated world and show a robot what to do once, and it remembers. Numbers are blocks you type on. Messages are birds who fly things to their nests. A program is a house with somebody working inside.

ToonTalk 3D is a from-scratch reimagining of those ideas in real-time 3D: a single HTML file, three.js, and a cast modelled by Blender scripts rather than by hand. Nothing of the original's artwork is copied — the robot, the vacuum, the Martian are all new. What is being copied, and argued with, is the semantics.

This is the log of how it went, and of the moment it changed direction.

The first three days were built from ToonTalk's reputation — from what its ideas are famous for. That gets you further than it should. By the end of day three the workshop had numbers as exact fractions, text pads, boxes with holes, a copier, robots trained by demonstration with thought bubbles, teams that take turns, birds and nests with real message-passing, a notebook, and rooms that hold whole worlds inside them.

Then we read the manual

On the third evening the work stopped and turned to two documents that had been sitting there the whole time: the original ToonTalk manual at toontalk.com, and the original C++ source. The output was not code but a file called DIVERGENCE.md — every difference found, each marked SAME, KEEP (a deliberate improvement), GAP (something real we lacked), or FIX (a misunderstanding).

The gaps were not decoration. They were the reason no real program could be written.

What we had built from memory

  • Scales that tip toward the larger number — and no way for a robot to notice which way.
  • Boxes that join, but never split.
  • No empty box, so recursion had no base case.
  • No source of randomness at all.
  • Each room's world bottled — serialised out, run, serialised back — because the engine assumed one world at a time.

What the manual said

  • A scale's tilt is the branch: robots match on it. That one omission was why nothing could make a decision.
  • A box dropped on the number N splits there — the array primitive.
  • A box with no holes is the empty array, the base case.
  • Randomness is a live sensor pad; ours became dice, which a robot can throw.
  • And, from the C++: there is one live world. Off-screen animations simply take zero time.

That last one was the deepest. The whole concurrency model of the original reduces to a single method:

if (showing_on_a_screen()) { ... return the_default_duration; }
return 0;                 // off-screen: every animation takes 0 ms

Houses in ToonTalk are not separate documents. They are places in one city, every robot stepped from one list, and the ones you are not watching run at zero seconds per move. Our bottling was an accident of architecture pretending to be a design. Within a day it was gone: rooms became real objects hanging inside the scene at toy scale, and a single interpreter turns to whichever world needs a moment.

The measurement, from the audit file: recursive fib(10) = 55, across 109 nested houses, in under three seconds. The bottling engine had stalled for minutes on fib(6).

Semantics unlocks, current engine — DONE. Proven the same day: a sentence factory, doubly-recursive Fibonacci, and a message-passing bank account with a dozing teller. DIVERGENCE.md, 19 August

Then we read his programs

A manual tells you what a language offers. It does not tell you how anyone actually writes in it. The third source of truth arrived when Ken handed over five of his own ToonTalk files — swap, factorial, n-to-1, append, reverse — saved out of ToonTalk 3 itself. A .tt file turns out to be a zip with an XML document inside, so they could be read move by move.

They were full of idioms no manual would think to mention:

Typing an operation is not typing a value

factorial takes a 1 from the stack and types a × on it. Our engine recorded that as "make it ×1" — an absolute value — so a robot taught on 30 would forever withdraw exactly 30. The fix was a new kind of remembered step: make it multiply, which works on whatever number the robot is handed.

Every program ends with a bomb

All five of Ken's programs finish by fetching a bomb and blowing up their own house. We have no bombs. The replacement is quieter and, we think, better: a robot vacuums the very box it was working on. Nothing matches any more, so the team stops for good — and a house with nobody working in it and nothing to work on folds itself away. Recursive programs now clear up after themselves.

A scale sits between two holes

In the original, a scale lives in a box and weighs the holes either side of it. Ours is a balance with two pans. Both of Ken's scale programs port cleanly by moving the compared numbers into the pans, and the difference is written down rather than papered over — which is what DIVERGENCE.md is for.

The lesson, learned twice

The last day added Marty the Martian, an AI helper who lives in the workshop. He answers questions and — deliberately — never writes programs for you, since doing it yourself is the entire point. When showing beats telling he gives a demonstration, driving the workshop through exactly the same clicks a person makes: walking to the stack, fetching a number, carrying it to the table.

Marty was given a careful system prompt describing the workshop. And Marty, fluently and confidently, began to make things up. Asked how to make one third, he explained you type 1, then a slash, then 3. You cannot. Asked whether a number could be seen as a decimal, he said the workshop keeps everything exact and never shows decimals — while the number blocks in front of him were displaying decimal faces with a bar over the repeating part.

I wonder if Marty needs to be provided with a textual version of the manual to base its answers on. Ken, 20 August

Which is, of course, the same sentence the project itself had needed two days earlier. Marty is now handed the manual — the real one, this app's own, twenty-one thousand characters of it — with every single question, and told to say he is unsure rather than invent. The hallucinations stopped, because they were never really hallucinations: he was doing what the project had done for three days, describing a workshop he had never read about.

The sixth day, and a wall

The last day went looking for somewhere to put the thing. A build script now packs the whole workshop — three.js, the loader, both Blender-made models as text — into a single 2.3 MB file that fetches nothing and runs from a disk, an email attachment or any static host. All eleven example worlds give byte-identical results in the packed build and the source, which is how it is checked.

A morning of probe pages went on finding out what a published Claude artifact will carry. That frame drops any inline script over about a megabyte without a word; it refuses to fetch anything at all, including a data: URL the page made itself. And then the wall: the workshop went white in it, markup and all, the moment its animation loop ran, at every boot delay tried. The conclusion written down that evening was that a live workshop is more than the frame will carry.

That conclusion was wrong, and it stood for three days. It came from one failing app and was generalised to the loop — the part of the sentence that was never tested on its own. On the seventh day a bare WebGL loop was published as an artifact and ran twenty thousand frames at a steady sixty a second, for six minutes, without dropping one. The packed 2.3 MB workshop was then published, and it runs. Whatever killed the first attempt was narrower than the loop and has still never been isolated — the honest record is that it is unknown, not that it is impossible.

Furniture, and who it belongs to

The rest of the day was the kind of correction only the person using it can make. Marty walked under the table with his antennae through the top. He clicked Mimi the copier from across the room, so the number appeared on her platform by itself. Your notebook came up in your hand when you meant to turn a page, and Dusty would vacuum it away altogether. Undo did not reach inside it, because a notebook travels through a snapshot by reference — so undo handed back the same book with the page already gone.

And the notebook, which remembers everything between visits, now belongs to a name: the workshop asks who is working here, and two people sharing a browser no longer share a library. Marty, Mimi, Dusty and Ruby moved to stand along the sides of the desk, because the opening view is the first thing anybody sees and half of them were under it or past its edge.

Half a million holes

Then Ken trained a robot to copy the box it was working on and join the copy back on. It is a perfectly ordinary thing to teach — it doubles a number, and it doubles the length of an array. He put it in a glass house and pulled the lever.

Twenty-odd rounds later the box had over 500,000 holes, and three separate layers had quietly given way, each in its own manner:

The room

The box burst the walls. A house mounts its world at a fixed miniature scale — a constant shrink, which cannot cope with contents that grow without bound. The box punched through both walls and dragged the house off across the floor. Rooms now measure what is inside them and shrink further when outgrown, never magnifying a small one back up.

The frame

The camera moved once every second or two. Every hole was building a pocket mesh and an anchor, so half a million holes meant a million objects for three.js to walk on every frame — all of it geometry nobody could see. A long box now draws its first and last six holes with a ⋯ N more ⋯ plate between, exactly as a tall pile on a nest already did.

The file

Saving it wrote 11 MB. Every empty hole was spelled out longhand. A box past sixty-four holes is now written sparsely: a hole count, and only the holes that hold something.

The measurements, on a 500,000-hole box holding a number at each end: 20 objects added to the scene rather than a million; 4.25 ms a frame against 4.61 ms for an empty workshop; the file down from 2.5 MB to 448 bytes, and its load from 1,916 ms to 334 ms. Both file shapes still read, so nothing saved before that day changed.

Version numbers, before they are needed

Updating the file format is fine. But for the future can we include version numbers in the files so changes won't break things. Ken, 21 August

Which is the request of somebody who has maintained a format before. Every world, thing, saved robot and notebook now records the format it was written in. An older file is migrated; a newer one is refused outright, naming both versions — rather than half-read into something that looks right and is quietly wrong, which is the failure the stamp exists to prevent. It cost nothing on the day it went in, and the sparse box was its first use.

Getting it wrong first

The room fix landed the wrong way round to begin with: the budget was written in the room's outer units rather than its inner ones, and since a bare robot already measures about 1.2 across on that inside scale, the very first version clamped every room — robot included, nothing having grown at all. Caught by measuring a room that had no business shrinking, which is the sort of check that only gets written when you have already been fooled twice.

A design, and then a tablet

The seventh day opened on the one gap in the list above that is not a missing feature but a missing idea. ToonTalk has sensors — live pads that both read and write a picture's x position — and they are the single place the language allows two robots to interfere with the same thing. Read x, compute, write x: each write is atomic, and the pair still races. Worse, sensors are not user-definable. A child can build a message interface on top of one, but only the substrate decides what gets to look like a sensor, and the substrate speaks Cartesian.

The morning went on arguing rather than typing, and the answer was to delete the feature. Every thing can be flipped over; its back is a little tabletop where its behaviour lives, and flipping yields a bird addressed to it. Messages are ordinary boxes with a selector pad in front. A back handles one message at a time, so no-concurrent- side-effects holds by construction rather than by a special rule for sensors. A gauge stops being a kind of thing and becomes an idiom: an ordinary number with a robot on its back keeping it current, and another dozing on its event nest to push changes back. The system's own x and y sensors become gauges the system happened to mint, from the same parts anyone else gets.

One rule then swallows another. A robot whose match fails on a live thing dozes on that thing's events — which is exactly the covered-nest rule, generalised, so there is one rule where there were two. The lineage is ask-suspension from concurrent constraint programming, and the write-up says so, along with the disanalogy that matters: that store is monotonic and this one is not, so instead of confluence the design promises two smaller things it can actually keep.

None of it was built. It is BACKS.md, costed at six to nine working days, in the order semantics-then-payoff. Writing the design down and stopping is a choice this project had not made before.

A licence, five days late

Then the housekeeping: MIT, because Apache-2.0's patent grant buys nothing here; a link to Ken's own account of the project; and a pointer to the copy on GitHub Pages for anyone who only wants to use it. Ken refreshed the repository page and the licence was not there — the files had been written and never committed. A small lesson about what “done” means, learned in public.

A brain that needs no key

Desktop Chrome carries a small Gemini inside it, and Marty can use it: no key, no account, nothing typed leaving the machine — which makes it the one brain a classroom can have without handing out a key. Two things had to be got right. Chrome will only begin the multi-gigabyte download from a real click, so the panel asks for it with a button of its own rather than setting it off from a child's first question. And the model is small: the manual is 22,500 characters and will not fit, so the manual's sections are scored against the question and only the nearest ones are sent.

Marty also stopped walking to an empty corner of the table before every demonstration — a trip that had been justified by a comment explaining why his props would otherwise land badly, when the code that places them never used his position in the first place. And he shows he is thinking: three dots circling over his antenna, because the little brain in Chrome is slow to wake the first time and a still Martian reads as a hung page.

The tablet

Then Ken opened it on a tablet, and the whole interaction model came apart in a way no desktop testing would ever have shown. A finger is not a pointer: it is only anywhere at all while it is touching, and while it is touching it covers the very thing it is over. What you were carrying followed it, invisibly, to wherever you last tapped.

So on a touch screen the carried thing no longer follows. It waits at a fixed spot on the screen where it can always be seen. The first attempt parked it on the working plane, and the clamp that keeps a carried thing over the bench dragged it around until it sat squarely behind a panel; it is fixed in the camera's frame now, so orbiting turns it in your hand rather than carrying it off. Nothing on screen is a text field, so a hidden one gives the keyboard something to appear over, and what it receives is turned back into key presses and handed to the ordinary typing rules — one set of rules, not two.

That last part was wrong twice. A phone keyboard does not hand you letters; it hands you its opinion of the word so far, over and over, and revises it when it changes its mind. Reading each event as newly typed turned hello into hhehelhellhello. Nothing is read out of the events now: what changed in the field is worked out by comparison, so a prediction that rewrites a whole word arrives as the backspaces and letters it really is.

What a robot should remember

The tablet session ended with a list of seven, and two of them were not interface problems at all.

Ken trained a robot to double a number — copy the thing it was given, combine the copy with the original — and did it by parking the copy on a work spot and bringing the original over to it. Running it, he was told the robot had swept its own box away: finished, for good, and his answer was gone. Both halves were wrong. An empty stand had been read as a robot announcing it was done, when it had merely put its work down somewhere else; and the routine that clears the work spots between runs deleted what was on them. An empty stand now reclaims from the work spots first, and work spots are cleared onto the table. Nothing in this workshop should make a thing cease to exist except Dusty.

Then: a robot trained by typing “Ken ” on a blank pad turned every pad it ever met into “Ken ”, where Ken expected it to keep adding. The codebase already agreed with him in the next function along — there is a comment there explaining that an operation typed on a number is its own step, because a robot shown “make it minus” must not remember “make it −30”. Writing on a pad had exactly the same shape and the opposite treatment. It records the addition now, and the file format went to v3 so an older build refuses the file rather than replaying it wrongly.

Right, and unreadable

The last report of the day was the most interesting, because nothing was broken. Take a result off the robot's stand, put it on the desk, press Undo — and it appears at the corner of the desk. Press Undo again and it returns to the stand. That is correct: putting something down is two moves, so undoing it is two, and the step between them is you holding it. But when you click Undo the pointer is on the Undo button, and the invisible hand draws what it carries at the pointer — so the thing was rendered low and near the front edge, which reads as “it wandered off” rather than “you are holding it”. It waits at the visible spot now, until the pointer next moves over the workshop, and says so. Being right is not the same as being legible.

How it worked
±

The loop

The working rhythm never varied. Ken plays with the workshop and sends a batch of observations — often with screenshots, usually five or six at a time, ranging from "the bird flew to the wrong room" to "eggs should be robin's-egg blue". Each one gets fixed, verified, and committed with a message that says what was actually wrong.

Verification is not by eye. The app exposes a development hook that can drive it headlessly — load a world, hand a box to a robot, run at instant speed, read the result — so every example is re-run after every change: swap gives 3/7, 5! = 120, reverse turns [1,2,3] into [3,2,1] across three houses that then fold themselves away.

Two honest failures

Twice the verification lied, and both times in the same way — the measurement was wrong, not the code. Chasing why Dusty the vacuum was invisible during a robot's clean-up, a cached index into the scene's object list went stale, "proving" he never moved when he had. In another run, Dusty had been left awake from an earlier probe, so a test click vacuumed the very thing it was meant to pick up. Both wasted more time than the bugs did.

The real answer, when it came, was better than the bug report: Dusty was flying — at floor level, straight under the table, arriving hidden behind it. He goes over the top now.

What is missing, on purpose and not

Deliberate departures

  • Dusty removes; Ruby erases. The original had one character with modes and a legend.
  • The copier is a machine on the floor, not a wand in your hand — so robots can use it exactly as you do.
  • Rooms are things: nestable, mailable, fileable. The original's houses are not.
  • mod and set are spelled out. Nothing says an operation must be one character.

Gaps still marked open

  • Operation-only pads: backspace the digits away and the bare operation remains.
  • Sensors — mouse, keyboard, time — the family that games are made of. Designed on the seventh day (BACKS.md) and deliberately not built.
  • Insertion-point editing in numbers and pads.
  • ToonTalk's puzzle game, which is how the original taught itself to a child without a manual.
  • Pictures, sounds and video as things you can hold — the reason the backs design exists, and the first thing it would buy.
  • Matching a robot's thought against a box of half a million holes still walks every one — per action, not per frame, but it walks them.

Every one of those lines is a row in DIVERGENCE.md, which is still the file the project argues with. It is the closest thing this workshop has to a conscience: a written record of where it departs from the thing it admires, and why.
