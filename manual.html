The basics

Moving around

Drag with the left mouse button held to orbit the camera around the workshop; scroll to zoom in and out (numbers show more digits and more of their faces up close); drag with the right button to slide sideways. On a touchpad: one-finger drag orbits, pinch zooms, two-finger drag slides.

Handling things

You do everything with single clicks: click a thing to pick it up, and it follows your pointer; click where you want it to set it down. The row of dark pedestals in a semicircle behind the robot's work area are stacks — endless supplies of numbers, text pads, boxes, scales, nests, rooms and little robots. Click a stack to take the top one.

The wooden table in front is your workbench. Robots can never reach it — that is the point: what you build there is yours until you hand it over.

Everything in the manual's little windows below is the real workshop — click things, carry them, try it.

Numbers

Numbers come off their stack as 1s. While holding one, type digits to set its value, - to make it negative, and * / ^ to choose what it does when dropped on another number (adding is the default — and adding a negative is subtraction). Dropping a number on a number combines them: the arriving one applies its operation. Division gives exact fractions, never rounded decimals.

A number block shows its value on the front and top; walk the camera close and the other faces show the same value in English words, scientific notation, and decimal form — with a bar over a repeating part when it fits. Fractions get words too: 1/6 reads one sixth, 3/4 three quarters, 7/2 seven halves.

Try it: drop the 6 on the ½ to add them. Pick up the ×3 and drop it on the result. Take fresh 1s from the stack and type digits while holding them.

Two operations have names rather than signs, because nothing says an operation has to be one character. Type mod on a held number and it will leave what is left over — 17 dropped on… rather, drop a mod 5 on 17 and 2 remains. Type set and it simply replaces whatever it lands on. The letters need not be typed cleanly: as soon as the last few you typed spell a name, the number takes it.

Text pads

Text pads are paper for words. Type on a held pad to write (Enter for a new line, Ctrl+V pastes). Drop one pad on the left edge of another and it reads first; on the right edge and it reads last — "Talk" dropped on the right of "Toon" makes "ToonTalk". A whole number dropped on a written pad shifts its first or last letter along the alphabet; on a blank pad, the number writes itself out as digits.

The other way round picks letters out: drop a pad on a whole number and that number chooses a letter — "Talk" dropped on a 2 leaves a pad reading "a". The number is spent saying which letter you meant.

Try it: pick up "Talk" and drop it on the right edge of "Toon". Take a 1 from the number stack, type 2, and drop it on a pad's left edge to shift a letter — or drop a pad on it to pick out letter 2.

Boxes and scales

Boxes have holes, and holes hold anything — numbers, pads, nests, other boxes, even little robots (they lie down inside). Type a digit while holding a box to give it that many holes; type 0 and you have an empty box, which joins onto another box without changing it. Boxes are arrays: drop a box on a box to join them — left half to put its holes in front, right half behind — and shrinking a box splits the extra holes off as a box of their own.

Drop a box on a whole number and it splits there: a box of five dropped on a 2 becomes a box of the first two and a box of the rest. That, with an empty box for the end, is how a robot walks along an array.

Scales have two pans that work exactly like holes; the beam tips toward the larger number. A robot trained on a scale that is saying something remembers the tilt, not the numbers: its thought reads "a scale tipping left", and it fires on any pair that leans that way — which is how a robot chooses between two paths.

Try it: put the 8 and 9 in the holes. Hold the box and type 4. Take a second box from the stack and join them.

A box with no holes is a mould. Drop a written pad onto one and it takes the word apart, one letter to a hole; drop a robot team on one and it comes apart, one robot to a hole (drop them on each other to make a team again); and pour a box of letters onto a blank pad to get the word back. A box that has holes is a container rather than a mould — dropping a robot into a hole is how a program carries a spare team — so the two never get in each other's way.

A scale weighs words as well as numbers: the one later in the alphabet is the heavier, and case is not weight, so "Apple" and "apple" balance.

Dice

Dice are where chance comes from. A die does not show a rolled number of its own — it re-rolls the number it lands on: drop a die on a 1 and the 1 becomes anything from 1 to 6, and the die is spent in the throw, like any pad you drop. To change the range, hold a die and type: type 20 and you have a twenty-sided one, and it says so on its faces.

Robots take dice from the stack exactly as you do, so "throw a die into hole 2" is an ordinary step. A team whose members expect different rolled numbers is a program that branches on chance — that is all the sentence factory does.

Try it: drop the die on the number and watch it change. Take a die from the stack, type 20 while holding it, and roll that one instead.

Birds and nests

Take a nest from its stack and set it down: the egg hatches into a bird — they are a pair for life. Give the bird anything and she flies it home to her nest, tucking new deliveries under the pile so the top is always the oldest. Click the top of a pile to take one delivery; grab the tower lower down and you pick up the whole nest, deliveries and all.

Copy a bird on the copier and both birds serve the same nest. Copy a nest and you have a broadcast: give any of their birds something and every copy of the nest receives its own copy at once.

Try it: wait for the egg to hatch, then drop the 3 on the bird and watch the delivery. Give her the 7 next — it goes under.

Two nests dropped together become one. The pile of the nest you are holding joins the pile below it, and the survivor answers to both names — so every bird of either nest goes on delivering, to here. That is how you rewire a program that is already running.

Robots

Take a mini robot from its stack, set it on the table, and drop something on it: it grows, steps behind the bench, and its thought bubble opens — you are now teaching it. Everything you make it do (take things, drop them, fetch from stacks, use the copier) is remembered as steps about its own things — "hole 1 of what it was given", never places in your world. Leave the bubble and the daydream rewinds; the robot keeps the lesson.

Its thought bubble shows the condition: the shape it must be given before it will run. Give a trained robot something that fits and it gets to work at once, repeating while the condition still matches, up to the Rounds limit. Stop does not freeze it mid-move: the round it is in finishes at once, so nothing is left half done, and Run carries on from there. Drop one trained robot on another to form a team: the members line up on the floor behind the leader, each with its own small thought over its head, and whichever one recognises what the team was given walks up to the desk and does the work while the others wait their turn.

A team stands in a queue — the leader at the front, the rest lined up behind it, on the table as well as at the desk. Nothing is hidden. Whatever you hand to a member is handed to the leader: a team has one leader and one work surface. Click a member to take it out of the team; set it down and it is a robot of its own again, or drop it on another robot to join that one. Drop a robot on the leader to add it to the end. Picking the leader up picks up the whole team.

How a robot says it is finished. A robot that vacuums away the very box it was working on has ended the team for good — there is nothing left for any thought to match, and the message says so rather than suggesting you erase anything. It is worth doing deliberately at the end of a program. A house whose robot has done that is a house with nobody working and nothing to work on, so it folds away to nothing, taking whatever is left inside with it. That is how a recursive program clears up after itself.

A robot in a thought bubble means that robot: one with the same name and the same lesson. Erase it with Ruby and it becomes "a robot — any one", and again "anything".

Typing on a number a robot is holding is remembered two ways, and the difference matters. Type digits and the robot remembers that exact number. Type only an operation — -, * and so on — and it remembers "make it subtract", which then works on whatever number it is handed. That is how one robot can take away 30 today and 7 tomorrow.

Click the bubble of a robot (big or mini) to take out a copy of the very thing it was first shown. Type while holding a robot to name it — the name appears on its chest. To save a robot, hold it and press Save held thing.

Try it: drop the 5 on the mini robot. In the thought bubble, click the number stack, then click a work spot; leave the bubble and it will run. Then erase its thought with Ruby and give it anything.

The little desks beside the robot are its work spots — its own scratch space, swept clean when it finishes. A spot sizes what you set on it the way a hole does: something too wide, a box of eight holes say, sits there smaller rather than hanging over its neighbours, and comes back to full size the moment it is picked up.

Mimi the copier

The tall machine at the right end of the arc is Mimi the copier. Set anything on her upper platform and she scans it; an independent, exact copy drops into the lower tray — contents, names and all. Click a surface with an empty hand to take from it (the tray first, then the original). Robots use Mimi exactly the way you do, which is how a robot copies things mid-program. She copies anything — a copied bird serves the same nest, a copied nest joins the flock so deliveries broadcast, and a copied robot arrives with its training, its name and its whole team. A robot that copies itself this way and sets the copy to work in a new room is a recursive program.

Try it: set the 7 on Mimi's top platform and watch the tray. Copy the box too — then put a number in one box and see the other stays empty: copies are independent.

Dusty and Ruby

Two helpers live in front of the table. Dusty the vacuum removes: click him to wake him, then click things to vacuum them away, entries off notebook pages, or parts of a robot's thought (leaving an empty hole, which then matches anything). Ruby the eraser generalises: wake her and click a part of a thought — a 7 becomes any number, then anything. She also erases real things in place: an erased number stands for any number, which is how you build a robot's expectations by hand. Waking one settles the other, and whoever is awake fidgets.

Rooms

Rooms are little houses that each hold an entire workshop. Drop a robot on a room and it steps inside, ready behind the desk. Drop anything else on the room and it lands on that desk — and the robot inside gets to work the moment your world goes quiet. The chimney smokes while somebody in there is actually working; a room merely holding unopened deliveries sits quiet.

Click the roof to switch between solid walls (the room works instantly, unseen) and glass (you watch it work, round by round, at your speed setting). The big lever on the side wall runs and stops the room: up, the robot inside works; down, it rests. Click the door to step inside: the room becomes the world around you, and the world you left shrinks onto the table as a little room of its own — walk back out through its door. If the room was running when you opened the door, its robot picks straight back up once it has grown to full size. Birds fly in and out of rooms to reach their nests, so a machine in a box can feed a nest on your table.

Robots can build rooms too. A robot may take a room from the stack, drop a robot in and something for it to work on, and stow the whole house in a hole of the box it is holding — a machine that builds machines. Rooms inside rooms work as well, all the way down.

Try it: drop the mini robot on the room, then the 4. Click the roof to make the walls glass. Click the door to step inside.

Marty the Martian

The little green fellow beside Dusty and Ruby is Marty. Click him and ask anything about the workshop — or say "show me birds" (or numbers, pads, boxes, dice, scales, robot, copier, dusty, rooms, notebook) and he demonstrates it right on your table, using the workshop exactly the way you do. A demonstration plays one narrated step at a time: Marty walks over to what he is showing, does one thing, and waits for you to click Next ▸ — Stop ends it, and so does asking him something else. When an answer of his suggests a demonstration, you get a Show me button rather than a surprise performance. He never builds programs for you — doing it yourself is the whole point.

Marty thinks with whatever brain he has. Without a key his replies are canned lines from a phrasebook — he can pick the right demonstration, but he is not really answering. For real conversation open How Marty thinks in his panel and choose a brain — Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini, and which model — then paste that provider's API key; it is kept only in this browser, and he then also sees what is on your table when he answers. Inside a Claude artifact he needs no key at all. The panel always says which of these he is using right now.

The notebook, saving and sharing

The notebook on your table is a two-page spread. Drop anything on a blank page to file it; click a filed entry to take out a copy (the page keeps its own). Drop a written text pad on a full page to open the first page mentioning that text; a whole number jumps to that page number. Only Dusty can remove an entry, and a notebook itself only goes when open at two blank pages. The first page holds a blank notebook — copy it out to make more. The main notebook remembers everything between visits, and it is furniture: it stays where it is when you load somebody else's world, because it is your library, not part of theirs.

Robots can use it too, which is what lets one program use another by name. A robot fetches a blank pad, writes a name on it, drops it on the notebook — which opens at whatever that name finds — and takes a copy off the page. That is the whole of the module system: the same three gestures you would make yourself.

Save held thing writes whatever is in your hand to a file — a box, a trained robot, a room with a whole workshop inside. Save world writes everything. Import file (or dropping a file onto the app) brings either back: things land in your hand, worlds replace the workshop.

Example worlds

Finished programs live in the examples folder. Download one and use Import file to load it. The last five are ports of Ken Kahn's own ToonTalk 3 programs, move for move.

The sentence factory. A glass room called the Scriptorium holds a team of twenty robots that roll dice to pick words, join text pads into sentences, and hand each one to a bird who flies it out to a nest on your table. Pull the room's lever and the nest fills with lines like "the frog dreams of the witch." Every mechanism in it is one from this manual: dice for the choice, team matching for the branch, pads for the words, a bird for the stream.

Fibonacci. Two of them: one that counts the leaves of the call tree, and one written exactly as the definition reads — answer 1 for 1 and 2, otherwise ask fib(n−1) and fib(n−2) and answer their sum, with each call getting its own house, its own nest and its own bird to reply on. A robot facing a bare nest simply dozes, so waiting for two answers needs no machinery at all. The leaf-counting one works like this: given a box holding a number, it drops a −1 on the number, copies its own box through Mimi twice, builds two rooms, puts a copy of itself in each, and carries both houses away in its own box — the two recursive calls. The robots that reach 1 or 2 mail a single 1 to a shared nest, so counting the letters counts the leaves of the call tree: for 8, twenty-one of them, from forty rooms nested six deep. A second robot then adds the pile up.

The bank account. The account sits on the Teller's desk inside a house, so nothing on the table can reach the balance — the only way in is to send a request to the bird. A request is a box holding an amount and a bird to reply to. The Teller weighs what the balance would be against nothing at all: tipping left or level he banks it and sends the new balance home with the request's own bird; tipping right he sends a "not enough money" slip instead. The scale is the whole decision, and also the whole state — with no scale on the desk only the robot that takes a request can match, and with one there only the three verdicts can.

The grammar. The sentence factory again, except the robots know nothing about nouns or verbs: the grammar is a dictionary box on the desk. A symbol is a word (a pad, said as it stands) or a rule (a number, looked up); each rule keeps its alternatives and a die with one face per alternative. Four robots run any language you write in those boxes — one splits the next symbol off the front, one joins a word onto the sentence, one expands a rule by splitting the dictionary at that number and throwing its die, and one sends the finished sentence off when nothing is left to say. Because the noun-phrase rule mentions itself, adjectives pile up: "pink silly big big boys kick silly silly dogs." Walk in through the door, edit the dictionary, and the language changes — no robot is retrained, because none of them ever knew it.

Sally's account. The same idea as the bank, but open on the table so you can see the whole of it, and message-driven: the account is [Requests, Balance, Owner] and the first hole is a nest. Drop a message like ["withdraw", 30] on the bird marked requests and she carries it there; the robot whose thought carries that word wakes, lifts the message off, does it, and dozes again — so the nest is bare between messages and the team waits. The dispatch is nothing but the pattern matching, and because the owner's name is in every thought, these robots serve Sally's account and no other.

Swap, and factorial. Two programs where a balance does the thinking. Swap is the smallest program here: its thought is "a scale leaning left", and it puts the two numbers the other way round — after which the scale leans the other way and it stops, because its thought no longer matches. Factorial is the same idea kept running: you hand the team [N, a bird] and the first robot builds the rest in front of you — a scale with 1 in one pan and N in the other, and "so far" joined onto the left of its own box. Then one robot counts up while the scale leans, and another answers when it comes level. Nothing counts rounds; the scale is the only test.

Lists: counting down, append, reverse. A list is the pair [first, the rest], a box with no holes is the empty list, and "the rest" is usually a nest — an answer that has not arrived yet. Counting down hands its list over one link at a time, keeping each new nest's bird so the next link lands inside the promise it just gave away. Append does the same to join two lists, which is why it keeps working when its first list is still being computed elsewhere. Reverse is the showpiece: for every link it sends a box and a copy of the append team into a house of its own, and each house dozes — smoking gently — until the nest it is watching fills. The last link is settled first, and the answer unwinds from the end of the list back to the front.
