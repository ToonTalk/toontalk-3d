The seven activities

How these activities work

These seven sessions are about a single question: how do you compare the sizes of collections that never end? They follow the Exploring Infinity sequence written for the original ToonTalk, rebuilt for this workshop. You will build everything out of a few parts:

  • Birds and nests. Take a nest from its stack and set it down: the egg hatches into a bird — a pair for life. Give the bird anything and she flies it home to her nest. A sequence, here, is a nest that keeps receiving numbers.
  • Robots. Take a mini robot from its stack, set it on the table, and drop a box on it: its thought bubble opens and you are teaching it. Do the job once; it remembers the steps and repeats them as long as what it is given still fits its thought.
  • Rooms. A robot on the open bench blocks the bench. Give each robot a room instead — drop the robot in, then its box — and pull the lever: rooms all work at once, and birds fly through their roofs. That is how you run a whole pipeline of robots together.
The one big idea

Imagine a room full of dancers, everyone dancing with exactly one partner. You can tell the two groups are the same size without counting either one. That trick — pairing instead of counting — is the only way to compare collections that never end, and every activity here turns on it.

Work in the pane on the right (or press Workshop above on a small screen). Marty — the little green Martian by the table — has been told which activity you are on: ask him anything, and he will nudge rather than answer. Each activity also has a finished world you can peek at when you are done — or stuck. Building your own first is the whole point.

Boxes are drawn here as [ In | Out ] — a box with two holes, the first labelled In, the second Out. To give a hole a label, write a text pad and drop it low on the front of the hole.

Start with Activity 1, on the left.

Activity 1 · Computing the even numbers in two ways

Challenge Can the same thing be made using all the numbers once — and also using every other number once?

Task ADoubling each term in a sequence

Train a robot called Doubler that takes each number arriving on a nest and gives a bird twice that number. Its box is [ In nest | Out bird ].

If you get stuck: training Doubler
  1. Take a nest from its stack and set it down; wait for the egg to hatch. Take a second nest and hatch it too — its bird is your Out bird; its nest is where answers will land.
  2. Take a box, and with it in hand press 2 for two holes. Put the first nest in the left hole and the second nest's bird in the right hole.
  3. The robot needs something to double while you teach it: drop a number on the first nest's bird so a delivery is sitting on the nest.
  4. Mini robot from its stack → set it on the table → drop the box on it. The thought bubble opens.
  5. Take the number off the nest and set it down on a free spot. Take a fresh number from the stack, press 2 then * — it is now a ×2 — and drop it on your number. Doubled.
  6. Pick the doubled number up and drop it on the bird. Click outside the bubble to finish.

Test it: give the In bird a number and watch the Out nest. Then a different number. Clear the Out nest, then give the In bird 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. What appears?

Now let's give Doubler all the natural numbers. We cannot type them all in, so a robot will make them: Add 1, whose box is [ number | Numbers bird ]. Each round he hands the bird a copy of his number, then adds 1 to it. Run him forever and the bird delivers 1, 2, 3, … forever.

If you get stuck: training Add 1
  1. Box with two holes: a number set to 1 in the left hole, a bird in the right (hatch a fresh nest; the nest is where the naturals will arrive).
  2. Drop the box on a fresh mini robot. In the bubble: take the number out of the left hole and set it on Mimi the copier's top platform. Click to take the copy from her tray, and drop the copy on the bird.
  3. Take the original back off Mimi and return it to the left hole.
  4. Take a fresh 1 from the number stack and drop it on the number in the left hole — dropping adds, so the count goes up. Finish.

Connect them: put the nest that receives Add 1's numbers into Doubler's In hole. Give each robot a room of its own (drop the robot into the room, then its box through the roof), set Rounds, and pull the levers.

Watch, then think

The first numbers coming out of Doubler — did you notice they are all even? Will there ever be an odd one?

Task BRemoving every other term

Train a robot called Split with the box [ Input nest | A bird | B bird ]. As numbers arrive, Split gives them alternately to bird A and bird B.

If you get stuck: Split needs no counter

The trick: always give the number to the bird in the middle hole — and then swap the two birds over, so next round the other one is standing there.

  1. Take the number off the Input nest, drop it on the bird in hole 2 (the middle).
  2. Take that bird out and set it on a free spot; move the bird from hole 3 into hole 2; put the parked bird into hole 3. Finish.

The swap is the alternation. There is no state anywhere except where the two birds are standing.

Feed Split the same natural numbers (copy Add 1's nest on Mimi — a copied nest joins the flock, so both copies receive every delivery). What kind of numbers end up on A's nest? On B's?

Task CTwo ways of making the same thing

Think

If all the robots ran forever, would there be any difference between the numbers on B's nest and the numbers on Doubler's Out nest?

If there is a difference — what is it? If there is none — how can they be the same sequence, when Doubler uses every natural number and Split sends only half of them to B?

Task DEven numbers dancing with natural numbers

If you saw a room full of children all dancing in pairs, could you tell — without counting — that there were as many on one side as the other? How would you convince someone?

Now imagine each number Add 1 hands out begins dancing with the number Doubler makes from it: 1 with 2, 2 with 4, 3 with 6, …

Think

If this went on forever, would every even number have a natural-number partner? Would every natural number have an even partner? What does that say about how many even numbers there are?

You may feel pulled both ways. So was Galileo, who wrote about exactly this in 1638. One line of reasoning says the sequences pair up perfectly; another says the evens are only half the naturals. Which do you believe — and what would you say to the other side?

The finished world has all three robots in rooms, fed by one Add 1, with B's nest and Doubler's nest side by side so you can watch them fill at the same rate.

Activity 2 · Combining infinite sequences — part 1

Claim Pat says there are more negative-and-positive whole numbers than just positive ones. Is Pat right?

Task ANegating each term

Train Negator, box [ In nest | Out bird ]: every number that arrives goes out with its sign flipped.

If you get stuck: training Negator

Just like Doubler, but the number you drop on is −1 ×: take a fresh number, press 1, then - to negate it, then * to make it a multiplier. Drop it on the incoming number, and give the result to the bird.

Connect Negator to the naturals from your Add 1 robot (Activity 1). Robots are connected by birds and nests.

Think

Will a positive number ever land on Negator's Out nest? Is there any negative integer that would never arrive, if the robots ran forever?

Task BTurning two sequences into one

Train Merge, box [ nest 1 | nest 2 | Out bird ]: it turns two sequences into one, taking numbers alternately — one from each.

If you get stuck: Merge is Split's twin

Same trick as Split, on the other side: always take from the first nest, then swap the two nests over.

  1. Take the number off nest 1, drop it on the bird.
  2. Swap the two nests: nest 1 to a free spot, nest 2 into hole 1, the parked nest into hole 2. Finish.

One more thing worth noticing once it runs: when the first nest is empty, Merge dozes rather than skipping ahead. Neither input can run in front of the other — the interleave is strictly fair.

Give Merge the nest receiving the positive integers and the nest receiving the negative ones.

Think

If Merge ran forever, would every integer come out? If any are missing — what could you do about it?

Task CIntegers dancing with natural numbers

Think

Merge's output contains every positive and every negative integer. Can you see a way to get all the natural numbers dancing with all the integers — every one with exactly one partner?

And what about 0 — who is it dancing with?

The finished world runs Add 1 → Negator → Merge in three rooms; the last nest fills 1, −1, 2, −2, 3, −3, …

Activity 3 · Generating different infinite sequences

Challenge Do all infinite sequences have the same size? What could "the size of an infinite sequence" even mean?

Task AInvent three sequences of your own

Train robots — reusing any you already have — to generate three infinite sequences, each different. Some ideas from the original sheet:

  • 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, … (square each natural)
  • 0.001, 0.002, 0.003, … (multiply each natural by 1/1000)
  • 1, −1/3, 1/5, −1/7, … (reciprocal, alternating sign)

Use fractions in at least one and negative numbers in another. Make one grow slowly and one grow fast. Be inventive!

If you get stuck: Squares, and fractions

Squares — take the number off the nest, set it down, copy it on Mimi, hold the copy and press *, drop it on the original: n × n. Give the result to the bird.

A fraction — there is no key for ½. Hold a fresh number, type the denominator, press /, and drop it on a number: it divides. 1 with a ÷1000 dropped on it is exactly 1/1000, no rounding.

For each sequence, write down the first five numbers, describe it in words, and say whether it contains any duplicates.

Task BSwap sequences with someone else

Save each sequence-making robot (Save held thing while holding it), and trade files with a classmate — memory sticks, shared drives and email all work. Import theirs with Import file, run them, and answer the same three questions about each: first five numbers, a description in words, any duplicates?

Task CA dancing partner for every term

Train Match Maker, box [ In nest | count | Dances bird ]. When a number arrives, Match Maker packs the little box [ count | number ], hands it to the bird, and counts up by one. If the count is 5 and −20 arrives, the bird gets [ 5 | −20 ].

If you get stuck: training Match Maker
  1. Take a fresh box, press 2, set it on a free spot.
  2. Copy the count (middle hole) on Mimi; put the copy in the little box's first hole.
  3. Take the number off the In nest; put it in the second hole.
  4. Give the little box to the bird.
  5. Fresh 1 from the stack, dropped on the count. Finish.

Run Match Maker on your own sequences and on the ones you imported.

Think

If the robots ran forever, would every term of the sequence end up dancing with a different natural number? Would every natural number get a partner?

Task DDo all infinite sequences have the same size?

Write your team's answer — on paper or a text pad — to these five, then argue it out with another team:

The questions

1. When can you say two infinite sequences have the same size?

2. Can you give two infinite sequences with different sizes? If not, why not?

3. If a sequence repeats itself, might it have only finitely many different terms? Example, or why not?

4. Can you describe sequences that cannot be programmed?

5. Are there infinite sequences with so many terms they cannot be counted at all?

Keep your answers — Activity 4 onward will test them. (Question 5 is the deepest one; the original sequence reaches it in an eighth activity, on Cantor's diagonal, not yet ported here.)

The finished world runs Squares into Match Maker: the pairs nest fills with [1|1], [2|4], [3|9], … — the pairing made visible.

Activity 4 · All the fractions between 0 and 1

Challenge Can we generate all the proper fractions?

Try listing them in order of size first — in your head. What is the very first fraction after 0? Whatever you name, a smaller one exists. There is no first step to take. So this activity walks a different way: by denominator — 1/2, then 1/3, 2/3, then 1/4, 2/4, 3/4, then 1/5, …

Task AAll the numerators for one denominator

Train Next Numerator. Its box holds a scale weighing the numerator (left pan) against the denominator (right pan), and a bird: [ scale | bird ]. While the numerator is smaller the scale tips right — and that tilt is the robot's condition. Each round it hands the bird the little box [ numerator | denominator ] and adds 1 to the numerator.

If you get stuck: training Next Numerator
  1. Take a scale from its stack. Put a 1 in the left pan and a 2 in the right — it tips right. Box with two holes: scale in the first, a bird in the second.
  2. Drop the box on a mini robot. In the bubble: fresh box, press 2, set it down; copy the left-pan number on Mimi into its first hole; copy the right-pan number into its second.
  3. Give the little box to the bird.
  4. Fresh 1 dropped on the left pan. Finish.

A robot trained on a tipping scale remembers the tilt, not the numbers — so it runs on any pair that leans that way, and stops by itself the moment the pans balance.

Test: put 1 and 100 on the scale and run. The robot should hand over [1|100], [2|100], … [99|100] — and stop on its own at 99. Why does it stop?

Task BPredict, test, generalise

Think

Predict: what sequence comes out if the right pan starts at 89 instead? Test: try it. Generalise: explain, in one sentence, what Next Numerator does for any starting pair.

Task CAnd then the next denominator

Train Next Denominator so the pair can go on forever: when the numerator has caught the denominator up and the scale balances, set the numerator back to 1 and take the denominator up one.

If you get stuck: the team, and the balance
  1. Train it on the balanced scale (put equal numbers in the pans first). Take a fresh 1 and, still holding it, type the word set; drop it on the left pan, and the pan is set to 1 rather than added to. Then a fresh 1 dropped plainly on the right pan: up one.
  2. Drop Next Denominator on Next Numerator: they form a team. The team tries its leader first; while the scale tips, Next Numerator runs, and the moment it balances, Next Numerator no longer matches — so the team falls through to Next Denominator.

Nobody counts anything. The scale is the entire conditional, and you can watch it tip.

Package the team in a room with its scale-and-bird box and run it. The bird should deliver [1|2], [1|3], [2|3], [1|4], [2|4], [3|4], [1|5] …

Think

If the team ran forever, would the bird ever be given two identical boxes? Would every proper fraction eventually be generated? How would you convince a friend — or if you think not, name a fraction that never comes.

Are any of the boxes equivalent to each other as fractions, even though the numbers differ? Give a few examples.

Task DFrom boxes to numbers — and the duplicates

Train Box to Number, box [ In nest | Out bird ], which takes each [a|b] box and gives the bird the single number a/b.

If you get stuck: division in place

Take the little box off the nest and set it down. Take the number out of its second hole, press / while holding it, and drop it on the number in the first hole: b divides a, exactly — the result is the fraction a/b. Give it to the bird, and let Dusty vacuum the emptied box away.

Watch the numbers arrive: 1/2, 1/3, 2/3, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4, … — there is the duplicate, wearing its simplified face.

Think

The original activity loads a ready-made No Copies team here that lets each fraction through only once. It is not ported yet — so think it instead: what would a robot need to remember to know that 2/4 has been seen before as 1/2? How could you build a memory out of boxes? (This is a genuinely hard build — a good stretch challenge.)

Task EDancing with the fractions

Think

Feed the fractions to Match Maker (Activity 3). Do you think all the fractions between 0 and 1 are countable? Write down how you would convince a younger child. What does countable mean, in your own words?

The finished world runs the All Fractions team into Box to Number; the fractions nest fills by denominator, duplicates and all.

Activity 5 · Rational numbers greater than 1

Claim Pat says: there must be more numbers between 1 and infinity than between 0 and 1. Is Pat right?

Task AThink before you build

Think

One way to make every rational above 1 is to redo all of Activity 4 with the comparison the other way round. But there is a much lazier way. Activity 4 made every fraction a/b with a smaller than b. What numbers do you get from b/a instead?

Task BOne robot does it

Train Divides 1, box [ In nest | Out bird ]: it takes each number n and gives the bird 1/n — the reciprocal.

If you get stuck: training Divides 1

Take a fresh 1 from the stack and set it down. Take the incoming number off the nest, press / while holding it, and drop it on the 1: the 1 becomes 1/n. Give it to the bird.

Feed it the fractions from Activity 4 and watch: 1/2 becomes 2, 2/3 becomes 3/2, 3/4 becomes 4/3 …

Think

Every fraction went in; what came out? Which numbers above 1 could never appear? Notice you did not just make the new set — you made it already holding hands with the old one: 1/2 with 2, 2/3 with 3/2. The pairing came free. What does that say about Pat's claim?

Sally is still uneasy: "There must be as many rationals from 1 to 2 as from 0 to 1. And from 2 to 3. And 3 to 4 — infinitely many intervals, each as big as (0,1). So there must be infinitely times more rationals above 1." Do you agree with Sally? Why, or why not?

The finished world runs All Fractions → Box to Number → Divides 1: the fractions and their reciprocals land on nests side by side, partner beside partner.

Activity 6 · Combining infinite sequences — part 2

Challenge Are all the rational numbers countable?

Task AAll the positive rationals

This is the capstone, and there is nothing new to train — it is made entirely of parts you already built:

  • All Fractions (Activity 4) — every fraction between 0 and 1
  • Box to Number (Activity 4) — [a|b] as the number a/b
  • Divides 1 (Activity 5) — each one turned over: the rationals above 1
  • Merge (Activity 2) — one from each side, in strict alternation

Wire them with birds and nests, a room each, and run. Remember Mimi: copying a nest makes a broadcast, so one stream of fractions can feed Merge directly and feed Divides 1 at the same time.

Think

If everything ran forever, would the number 1 be in the merged sequence? Would it be there more than once?

Task BAll the rationals

Use Merge again, and a robot like Negator (Activity 2), to produce all the rationals — positive and negative.

Think

So: can the rational numbers be counted? The rationals are dense — between any two there are infinitely many more, and no rational has a "next one". And yet look at your last nest: there they all are, in a queue, arriving one at a time, every single one after finitely many steps. If you think that settles it, how would you convince someone? If not, what is still missing?

The finished world is five rooms and one lever: All Fractions → Box to Number → Divides 1 → Merge, every positive rational on the last nest.

Activity 7 · Rationals between any two numbers

Claim Pat says: there are infinitely many rational numbers between any two rationals — however close. Is Pat right?

Task ADouble every fraction

Take the sequence of all fractions between 0 and 1 (Activity 4, through Box to Number) and connect your Doubler from Activity 1.

Think

Where do all the doubled fractions land? Any number bigger than 2 in the sequence? Any negative ones?

Joe says: "Some rationals between 0 and 2 must be missing, because doubling thins the numbers out." Is he right? If so, name a missing number. If not — what is wrong with "thinning"? (Every output came from exactly one input…)

And the mirror question: if a robot halved a sequence with no duplicates, could the result have duplicates?

Task BPredict · test · generalise

Train an Add 10 robot — each incoming number plus 10 — and feed it the fractions.

Think

Predict: describe the sequence before you run it. Test: run it. Generalise: what do you get multiplying by 1000 instead? By 0.5? By any number at all? Adding −0.5? What is the recipe for landing all the fractions in exactly the interval you want?

If you get stuck: Halve, without a fraction key

To multiply by a half: take a fresh number, press 2 then /, and drop it on the incoming number — dividing by 2 is multiplying by ½, exactly.

Task CAny interval at all — the stretch challenge

Train an Interval robot with box [ In nest | A | B | Out bird ]: for each incoming fraction x it computes A + (B − A) × x and hands it out — every fraction between 0 and 1 relanded between A and B, whatever A and B are.

Think

Between 10 and 10.001, then: how many rationals? Was Pat right? However short the interval, what exactly lives inside it?

The finished world feeds one stream of fractions to three rooms at once — ×2, +10, ÷2 — and the three nests fill between 0 and 2, 10 and 11, 0 and ½, all at the same rate.
